COINTELPRO — The FBI ran a covert programme to infiltrate, discredit, and break up domestic political organisations, forging letters and leaking material to the press to turn members against each other.
MKUltra — The CIA dosed unwitting subjects with LSD, ran safe houses through a front called Operation Midnight Climax, and ordered the files destroyed before Congress could read them.
Tuskegee — Public health officials withheld penicillin from black men with syphilis for forty years and kept the study running after a cure was standard.
Gulf of Tonkin — The administration pressed a second attack that had not happened into a congressional resolution, and the officials involved knew the sonar reports were doubtful.
Operation Northwoods — The Joint Chiefs drafted a plan to stage attacks on American targets and blame Cuba for them, and it went as far as the Secretary of Defense before it was rejected.
Operation Paperclip — The United States quietly brought in German scientists with Nazi party records, laundered their files, and put them to work on rockets and aviation medicine.
Operation Ajax — The CIA and MI6 organised street mobs, bribed officers, and placed newspaper stories to remove Iran's prime minister in 1953.
Operation Condor — South American intelligence services coordinated the abduction and killing of exiled opponents across borders, sharing lists and detention sites.
Church Committee — A Senate inquiry found that intelligence agencies had opened mail, tapped citizens, and plotted the assassination of foreign leaders for two decades.
Watergate — The president's staff ran burglaries and wiretaps against political opponents, then used the CIA to try to stop the FBI investigating it.
PRISM — The NSA collected communications in bulk from major internet companies under orders from a court whose rulings were themselves secret.
ECHELON — Allied signals agencies shared intercepts with one another to sidestep the laws restricting what each could collect on its own citizens.
Iran-Contra — Officials sold missiles to Iran and diverted the proceeds to fund insurgents in Nicaragua that Congress had explicitly refused to finance.
Tobacco industry — Cigarette makers knew nicotine was addictive and their own scientists had linked smoking to cancer, and they funded decades of doubt to keep it contested.
Exxon and climate — Company researchers modelled the warming from fossil fuels in the seventies, and the company then paid for public campaigns arguing the science was unsettled.
Purdue Pharma — The company trained sales staff to tell doctors that its opioid was less addictive than it was, and kept selling after it knew where the pills were going.
Volkswagen — Engineers wrote software that detected an emissions test and changed engine behaviour to pass it, and the cars polluted many times over the limit on the road.
Libor — Traders at several banks coordinated their submissions by chat message to move a benchmark rate that priced trillions in contracts.
Sugar Research Foundation — The industry paid Harvard researchers to write a review that shifted the blame for heart disease from sugar to fat, and the funding was not disclosed.
Flint water — Officials switched the city's water source, ignored the corrosion the switch caused, and told residents the water was safe while lead levels climbed.
Bhopal — The plant's safety systems were shut down or broken before the leak, and the company fought for decades to keep the liability off its books.
Radium girls — The company's own doctors examined the dial painters and attributed their jaw necrosis to syphilis to protect the firm from claims.
Guatemala 1954 — A US-backed operation removed an elected government on behalf of a fruit company's landholdings, using a fake radio station and a handful of aircraft.
Chile 1973 — American agencies funded strikes, propaganda, and officers in Chile ahead of the coup, and the documents saying so were declassified decades later.
Lavon Affair — Israeli military intelligence planted bombs in Egyptian and American buildings in Cairo, intending the attacks to be blamed on local militants.
Gladio — NATO countries kept armed stay-behind networks after the war, and in Italy parts of that network were entangled with domestic bombings.
Rainbow Warrior — French agents attached limpet mines to a Greenpeace ship in Auckland harbour, killing a photographer, and the government denied it until two of them were caught.
Hillsborough — Police altered their own officers' statements after the disaster and briefed the press that the fans had caused it.
Catholic Church abuse — Dioceses moved priests between parishes after complaints, kept the records in sealed archives, and settled with families under confidentiality clauses.
Epstein network — A convicted trafficker received a secret non-prosecution agreement that also shielded unnamed co-conspirators, and the victims were not told before it was signed.
Wirecard — Executives invented a large cash balance in Asian escrow accounts, and auditors signed the accounts for years without confirming the money existed.
Enron — The company moved losses into partnerships it controlled, and its auditor shredded documents when the investigation began.
Theranos — The company ran its demonstrations on other manufacturers' machines while telling investors and a pharmacy chain that its own device was performing the tests.
Cambridge Analytica — A contractor harvested profile data through a quiz app and used it to build targeting models for political campaigns without the users' knowledge.
FIFA — Executive committee members took bribes for tournament votes over two decades, and the payments ran through accounts in several jurisdictions.
Russian doping — A state laboratory swapped athletes' urine samples through a hole in the wall during the Sochi games, with the security service handling the bottles.
Operation Sea-Spray — The Navy sprayed bacteria over San Francisco to study aerosol dispersal and did not tell the city.
Unit 731 — Japanese researchers ran lethal human experiments in occupied Manchuria and were given immunity by the United States in exchange for their data.
Plutonium injections — Manhattan Project doctors injected hospital patients with plutonium without consent to measure how the body handled it.
Nayirah testimony — A public relations firm coached the daughter of Kuwait's ambassador to testify to Congress about atrocities she had not seen.
Salt Typhoon — State-linked intruders sat inside telecom networks for months, reading the lawful-intercept systems built for surveillance in the first place.
Pegasus — A commercial spyware firm sold governments a tool that took over phones without a click, and it was used against journalists and lawyers.
Snowden disclosures — An NSA contractor copied documents showing bulk collection of domestic call records and handed them to journalists rather than to an inspector general.
Pentagon Papers — A classified history showed that four administrations had privately concluded the war was unwinnable while saying otherwise in public.
Stuxnet — Two states wrote malware that spun Iranian centrifuges to destruction while reporting normal readings to the operators.
Gulf War sarin exposure — The Defense Department knew troops had been near a demolished chemical depot and took years to tell them.
